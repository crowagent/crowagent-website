What we have

One shared database in Ireland. Row-level security on the buyer product. Hand-written filters on the supplier product. A seam that can record an isolation decision, holding none.

What the gap is

Five of the six things needed to put a customer on its own database do not exist. The supplier product has no seam at all. The region is not the UK, and bid content leaves it.

What it costs

A second London project is roughly $10 to $60 a month. Cyber Essentials is £320 plus VAT and was deferred by owner decision on 17 August 2026 until a live public sector opportunity needs it, so nothing has been bought. The build is 6 to 10 weeks, my estimate.

What to do next

One London database shared by all UK-residency customers, not one database per customer. It answers the same procurement question for one extra database rather than N.

The finding that decides itThe decision is recorded now, and only one value is reachable

The seam that chooses a tenant's database is read on every buyer request by seventeen non-test consumers, sixteen buyer API routes and one page (re-counted 18 August 2026, up from twelve). It reads one table. Until 17 August 2026 that table held zero rows against thirteen production organisations, so every tenant resolved to pooled by fallback rather than by a decision anyone had recorded. The catalogue is now backfilled, ten organisations on staging and thirteen on production, with a trigger so it cannot empty again on the next signup. Every row records pooled, because pooled is the only value the code can honour.

Evidence: the seam, the catalog, and why promotion is not a configuration change

resolveTenantDatasource(orgId, sessionClient) is described in its own header as the one seam through which every buyer database access flows. It reads public.tenant_datasource_catalog, carrying organisation_id, isolation_tier, datasource_ref, connection_secret_ref and region. It runs under the caller's RLS session client and never a service-role bypass, the catalog's own policy exposes only the caller's organisation row, and the catalog schema refuses to hold a raw connection string: a future dedicated tenant may hold only a secrets-manager key. A control-plane read failure falls back to pooled rather than failing the request. This is careful work.

It is not a control that reaches nothing. Twelve non-test files consume the seam, plus the module itself: ten route files under web/app/api/council/ and two pages under web/app/(dashboard)/public-sector/. Nothing in the codebase can set a tenant to dedicated. The string "dedicated" appears exactly twice in all shipping code across web, portal and api: once in the type union, once in the guard that throws. The only writer of isolation_tier anywhere is the owner-gated provisioning route, and it hard-codes 'pooled' with datasource_ref: 'supabase:primary'. A gap in the guard, found in this review. app/api/council/buyer-profile/route.ts performs a real database read by calling loadBuyerProfile(guard.supabase, orgId), passing the raw session client rather than the resolved ds.client. Putting one customer on its own database needs six things. Two of them exist.

The seam is real, careful and better wired than the handover claimed. The catalogue that feeds it now holds a decision for every organisation. It is still one link in a chain, and the other four links have not been built. This is why promotion is not a configuration change: recording a decision the runtime cannot honour changes nothing.

Evidence: what a shippable silo would still need, beyond the six
  • A datasource runtime: a client factory per datasource_ref, connection pooling per tenant, and secret resolution from a manager we do not currently run.
  • A provisioning path that creates the project, replays all 653 migrations, seeds reference data, writes the catalog row, and can roll back a half-provisioned tenant.
  • Migration fan-out: every future migration applied to staging, to the pooled primary, and to each silo, with per-tenant schema-version tracking.
  • The same seam on the FastAPI tier, which has none at all.
  • Per-tenant backups, restore drills, alerting, log routing and cost attribution.
  • Cross-tenant reporting, which stops being one query.

Reasoning rather than citation: that list is derived from the code as it stands, not from a vendor document. The vendor evidence for why each item is expensive is in the market appendix at the foot of this page.

The gap that matters is not the database

Four things will stop a public sector or enterprise sale. None of them is solved by giving a customer its own database, and the most expensive of them is the one most easily mistaken for a database problem.

Blocker 1

Data is in Ireland, not the UK

Every row sits in AWS eu-west-1. The published residency page states in bold that no customer data is stored in a UK region and that region pinning is not offered on any plan.

Evidence and the exact G-Cloud consequence

The tenant-model migration is candid about this in its own header, and the published residency page is more so. It states that all customer data today is stored in AWS eu-west-1, that no customer data is stored in a UK region, that region pinning is not offered, and that a UK storage region would be a future change requiring engineering work and a separate written agreement. That page is correct and should not be softened. It means the honest answer to the G-Cloud field "Data storage and processing locations" is today European Economic Area, not United Kingdom.

Blocker 2

Bid content leaves the UK and the EEA

The same page discloses Anthropic in the United States and the Gemini API as "not region-pinned by the API used". A tenant database in London would not change this.

Why this outranks the database question

Government policy does not impose UK-only storage. Individual departments impose it by contract instead. DWP's contractor policy states that authority assets "must not be accessed, processed, transmitted and/or stored outside of the United Kingdom without the prior written consent of the Authority", and its definition of offshoring catches non-UK staff accessing data and diagnostic data sent abroad, not only where the database sits. A residency clause we sign would bind our support rota, our logs, our backups and our sub-processors. That is where the AI path becomes the binding problem rather than the database.

Blocker 4

The supplier product has no database-level separation

It runs entirely on a key that bypasses row-level security, so isolation rests on hand-written filters in application code. This is the finding with the widest blast radius.

The confidentiality question a supplier will ask, answered in full

A supplier's bid, tender and contract content is commercially sensitive, and that supplier's competitors may be other tenants on the same platform. "Trust our filters" is not an answer a procurement lead will accept. What is true today:

  • No supplier tenant can read another's data through any shipped route. Every supplier route resolves the organisation from server-trusted membership, never from the request body, and a body-supplied organisation that mismatches the verified one is rejected. (api/app/dependencies/service_auth.py:85-141)
  • Buyer and supplier data are in different tables and neither product's code reads the other's, verified in both tiers.
  • There is one deliberate cross-tenant read, and it should be disclosed rather than discovered. The PPN 002 benchmark endpoint selects across all organisations' completed contracts to produce sector aggregates. It reads id, contract_value, sector, social_value_score from crowmark_contracts and full rows from crowmark_measures for a sample, aggregates them, and returns only aggregates. It is feature-gated on crowmark.benchmarks, capped, and the code comments are candid that RLS could not have bounded it. No bid narrative text is read. (api/app/routers/crowmark/analytics.py:135,181)

A supplier asking "can my competitors see my data?" deserves the whole answer. Today the whole answer includes: your completed contract values and social-value scores contribute to a benchmark other tenants can see in aggregate, and your bid content is sent to AI sub-processors in the United States and to an endpoint that is not region-pinned. Both are defensible. Neither is currently something the customer is asked to consent to at the point it matters.

Why a dedicated instance is often the wrong answer to the question being asked

When a customer asks for "our own database", they are usually managing one of four risks: another tenant reading their data, us reading their data, data leaving the jurisdiction, or not being able to get their data back. Only the first is addressed by a silo, and it is already addressed for the buyer product by RLS and for both products by server-trusted scoping. The other three are answered by a contract, a region, an encryption control and an export guarantee. Offering a silo in response to a residency question is expensive misdirection: it costs us a fleet and leaves the customer's real objection standing.

For a company with no customers yet, committing to N databases before the first contract is the most expensive promise available.

A database per tenant, and what it multiplies

This is what most customers mean when they ask for their own database. It is buildable and the compute is cheap. Everything around the compute is what costs, and it scales with the number of tenants rather than with revenue.

Evidence: what silo costs, in AWS and Microsoft's own words

AWS names three models. Silo gives each tenant dedicated resources, for example a separate database. Pool shares resources, which AWS calls "the more classic notion of multi-tenancy". Bridge acknowledges that most real systems are mixed, some components siloed and some pooled. AWS adds a qualifier that matters for us: even a silo still runs a shared identity, onboarding and operational experience, and that is what separates SaaS from a managed service where each customer runs their own version.

What CrowAgent would have to write today

All customers share one PostgreSQL database. Every request resolves the caller's organisation from server-held membership records, never from the request body, and a mismatched organisation identifier is rejected. On the buyer product, reads run under a per-session database role and row-level security restricts every row to the caller's organisation; 515 policies cover 309 tables. On the supplier product, separation is enforced in application code rather than by the database, because that tier connects with a privileged key. One reporting endpoint returns cross-customer aggregates and no customer content. Data is stored in AWS eu-west-1 (Ireland). No dedicated instance is offered.

99 words. Written to be true rather than to score well.

Two sentences in that answer are the ones a reviewer stops on: the supplier-tier sentence and the Ireland sentence. Tier 2 rewrites the second. Giving the FastAPI tier an RLS-respecting path, or a defensible equivalent, rewrites the first. A dedicated database per tenant rewrites neither more cheaply.

What large private enterprises ask for instead

  • SOC 2 Type II report, and often a recent third-party penetration test. CREST accreditation is the usual name; NCSC's CHECK scheme is the public sector equivalent. Note NCSC's own caveat: a pen test "can only validate that your organisation's IT systems are not vulnerable to known issues on the day of the test".
  • SSO via SAML, and SCIM for provisioning. SAML gets authentication; SCIM is what closes the leaver-deprovisioning gap, which is what reviewers test.
  • Customer-managed keys (BYOK, CMEK). It is server-side encryption at rest under a key the customer can rotate, disable or destroy, which gives crypto-shredding and auditable key use. It does not protect against exposure through the application, which holds decrypt rights while serving a request. That last point is my inference from the scope of the vendor documentation, not a quoted vendor statement.
  • Contractual controls: a DPA meeting UK GDPR Article 28(3), a sub-processor list with a notice-and-object right under Article 28(2), and a breach notification clause. The ICO deadline to the regulator is 72 hours; as a processor our contractual clock to the customer is normally shorter, commonly 24 to 48 hours, to leave the controller time to file.
  • Residency as a contract term. The market-standard formulation is a commitment with carve-outs, as in AWS's "We will not move or replicate your content outside of your chosen AWS Region(s) without your agreement".

D8 Do we set a floor price for tier 3? The compute is $15 a month. The operational load is not. A silo sold at compute cost is a loss-making promise that lasts as long as the contract.

Direct connections plateau at 500 from 12XL upward while pooler clients scale to 12,000. What a dedicated instance costs elsewhere in the market

The "dedicated means enterprise, contact sales" convention is not universal. MongoDB Atlas sells dedicated clusters self-serve from $0.08 per hour, around $57 per month, with a published price list.
