Section 6

Seven Decisions, With Their Trade-offs

Nothing below has been implemented. Where a genuine commercial choice exists, both options are given with what each costs. Where one option is clearly dominated, that is said plainly — and where I have a view, it is labelled as a view.

Decision A  Portfolio: publish or withdraw the price

This one has an answer already recorded — R241-PRICING-STRATEGY 3 says contact-sales, never publish £549 — so the real question is whether you still hold that view.

Hold the decision (cheapest): filter Portfolio out of the public pricing API response, as the platform’s own pricing page already does. One code change, one surface, no commercial change.

Reverse the decision: publish £549 / £5,929 on the website, drop the quote-only FAQ, remove the test pin. Gains a self-serve path to the top tier; loses the ability to price an org-scoped rollout to the org.

Doing neither leaves an anonymous endpoint contradicting your recorded decision.

Decision B  Buyer and Public Sector: sell them or archive them

Nine live prices, up to £21,589/yr, reachable by nobody. The Buyer set carries deliberate metadata (R262-BUYER-001, seat and project counts), so it was built, not spilled.

Sell: they must reach the public API, the pricing page and the portal matrix, and the entitlement work must finish. That is real product work, not a config change.

Archive: costs nothing today — no live Stripe subscription depends on any of them. The one Public Sector grant on production is a manual grant, not a Stripe subscription, so archiving the price does not disturb it.

My view, offered as a view: archiving is reversible and free; leaving them live is the only option that carries risk with no revenue.

Decision C  The two prices that cannot do automatic tax

crowagent_council_standard_annual (£9,600) and crowagent_council_county_annual (£19,200) carry tax_behavior: "unspecified", which automatic_tax rejects. The field is immutable — they can only be superseded, never patched.

council_small_annual is correctly exclusive. That control is what makes this drift rather than a house convention.

C is downstream of B and of E. If the ladder is archived or re-cut, new prices get created correctly and this disappears without a remediation.

Decision D  Finish the seat correction, or accept the gaps

Four things still say something other than 1 and 5. Only two of them need your decision:

Yours: the marketing site advertises 3 and 10, next to the sentence “These are the numbers the platform enforces.” — and the Terms of Service promise a trial for “up to 3 users”. Either the copy and the terms come down to 1 and 5, or the caps go back up. There is no third state that is honest.

Yours: the portal grants Starter beta invites 2 seats. Is a beta partner deliberately given more than a paying Starter customer, or is that a leftover?

The pressure valve that makes 1 seat workable is not the reviewer pool. The canonical spec is explicit that a viewer does consume a seat, because that role holds approve_work — recording an approve/reject decision on a bid is doing the job the product exists for, so it is not a free seat.

The actual valve is the guest review invite (crowmark_review_guest_invites): token-hashed, contract-scoped, expiring, and with no org_users row, so it genuinely sits outside the tenant. That is how a solo bidder gets a colleague’s eyes on a draft without a second seat. There is no cap on it in code today — and an uncapped guest mechanism is an uncapped seat mechanism. The spec proposes 3 / 10 / 25 per contract, expiring at 30 days (REQ-PRICE-018).

Related, and yours: the spec argues seat add-ons must not be sold on Starter, because Starter + 2 seats at £45 = £139 undercuts Pro at £149 while carrying 550 fewer credits. Worth confirming, since it constrains how 1-seat Starter can be softened.

Mine to fix, not yours: the stale comments and the §3.6 spec text. Those are cleanup.

🧭 A structural observation, separate from the choice

There is a strong argument that “Public Sector” should not be a product ladder at all. Measured, it is not really a different product from CrowMark Buyer — both are the buy-side of a procurement, both are advisory, and both size on seats plus assessment volume. The only genuine differences are the billing mechanics (annual, invoice/PO, no self-serve checkout) and the regulatory content (PPN 002, social value).

Those are a payment method and a content pack, not a product line. Collapsing Public Sector into the Buyer ladder with an invoice-billing flag would delete an entire duplicated axis — its enum values, its seat row, its credit row, its PLAN_LIMITS keys and three Stripe prices — rather than renaming it.

The canonical spec has already reached this conclusion independently. Its §6.1 matrix collapses both into one Buyer S / M / L row spanning public and private sector, and states the reason directly: the supplier ladder must not vary by sector, because that “removes an entire class of pricing bug — there is no second supplier ladder to drift.” Two independent passes arriving at the same structure is weak evidence, but it is evidence.

I am still not recommending it — it is a bigger call than E, and it only pays off if the answer to B is “sell the Buyer ladder”.

🔢 Decision F — sequencing, so nothing is paid for twice

These decisions are not independent, and taking them in the wrong order means creating Stripe prices you then have to supersede.

1. Answer B (sell or archive Buyer / Public Sector). Everything else hangs off it.
2. If “sell”, answer E before any Stripe write — the ladder shape decides how many prices exist.
3. C resolves itself under either answer: archive, or create new prices with tax_behavior set correctly the first time.
4. A and D are independent of all of the above and can be done today.

The one free action available immediately, under every branch: rename the Stripe product “CrowMark Council” and its local-authority-only description. That string reaches invoices, name is mutable, and no branch of any decision keeps it.

Decision G  Is Free a plan, or a state?

The platform ships a Free tier with a real seat count and a real credit allowance. The website says there is no free plan. Both are internally consistent; together they are not.

Free is a plan: publish it — 1 user, 15 AI credits — and it becomes a top-of-funnel entry point that costs roughly £0.30 a month per org — 15 credits at Starter’s own modelled unit cost of £4.00 ÷ 200; my arithmetic, not a measured figure. Risks diluting the 14-day trial, which currently does that job with 50 credits and a card on file.

Free is a state: it is simply what an org looks like before it subscribes. Keep it unpublished, and the website copy is already correct. Then the 15-credit allowance needs a stated purpose, or it is a quantity nobody chose.

Either answer is cheap. This is on the list only because leaving it unanswered is how the seat figures drifted in the first place — a number in a table that no decision stands behind.

How This Was Measured, And What Is Deliberately Not Claimed

🔬 Controls run — because an empty result proves nothing on its own

Stripe completeness. GET /v1/prices?active=true&limit=100 returned 19 with has_more: false, so the catalogue is not a page-1 sample.

Stripe read path works. A single subscription id read failed with No such subscription. That alone proves nothing, so GET /v1/subscriptions?status=all was run as the control — it returned data, has_more: false. The read path works; the id genuinely is not in the live account.

Grep controls. Every “this appears nowhere” claim was paired with a search proven to return non-zero hits with the same tool. The seats_limit single-reader result was controlled with seats_used; the “Public Sector” sweep was controlled against the council sweep.

Seat-safety cross-check. The claim “no org exceeds one seat” was re-run independently against production rather than taken from the code comment asserting it. It holds: max = 1.

Website extraction. The live page was fetched with a cache-buster (HTTP 200, 75,400 bytes), scripts and styles stripped, and text extracted — then cross-checked against an independent sweep of the website repository. The two agree on every figure.

A trap that would have produced a false clean result. The website repo’s .gitignore ignores astro/dist/, and the search tool honours .gitignore — so the deployed build is invisible to a default search. Every claim about what is actually served was re-run against an explicit astro/dist path. Searching only the source would have been searching the wrong artefact.

🚫 Not claimed — named rather than quietly omitted

No checkout was ever exercised. Not for Portfolio, not for Buyer, not for Public Sector. Every statement is that a price exists and is active, never that a purchase completes.

Stripe was READ ONLY. Nothing was created, updated or archived.

The five non-live subscription ids were not traced to an account. They are provably not in acct_1TB3MxILkzuI7dCV; which account they belong to was not established, and no claim is made that they are test-mode rows.

Credit weights were not audited. The margin percentages are the packaging document’s own arithmetic, reproduced, not independently re-derived from live AI spend.

No customer count is asserted for any tier beyond the row counts stated.

The website extraction reads rendered text only. A figure that appears solely inside an image or a script payload would not have been seen.

Whether the §10 credit question was ever answered could not be determined — only that no record of an answer was found. Absence of a record is not proof of absence of a decision.

Two of my own findings were wrong, and are corrected in place rather than deleted. I first treated PRICING-AND-PACKAGING-2026-07.md as canonical when it has been superseded; and I asserted that the Public Sector tier ladder still showed “County” to customers when the display layer already says “Large”. Both errors came from reading keys and specs instead of the rendered artefact. They are marked as corrections on this page because the failure mode is more useful to you than a clean-looking result.

I did not audit the AI prompt outputs — only the prompt text. No claim is made about how strongly “UK local-authority” framing actually shapes a generated award letter.

The page was not visually rendered in a browser. It was verified structurally: HTTP 200 from the server, exact tag balance globally and within the tab, and every CSS class used proven to exist.

📌 The conclusion, in one paragraph

The supplier price ladder — Starter and Pro — is in genuine four-way parity and needs no decision. Everything above and beside it is unresolved: a top tier whose price one surface publishes against a recorded decision, two ladders that exist only where money is taken, a seat number the public site over-promises, credit allowances that were implemented before they were accepted, and a buy-side product named after a single customer type. None of it is expensive to fix today, because there is no live subscription revenue to protect. All of it becomes expensive the day there is.
